# Match the anchor star to the planets

> **Decision record (2026-10-30).** This research report recommends making the
> anchor follow the planets by default. The owner instead kept Moira's
> established astrometric anchor as the default and made the apparent anchor an
> explicit choice: the `Ayanamsa.*_APPARENT` systems, for example
> `"True Chitrapaksha (apparent)"`. See
> [the fixed-stars backend standard](../../02_standards/FIXED_STARS_BACKEND_STANDARD.md),
> Section 5.0. The report below is unchanged. The nutation leak it flags as a
> possible concern does not occur: Moira refers both the star and the planets to
> the true ecliptic and equinox of date.
>
> **Addendum:** [the original-text reading](AYANAMSA_ANCHORS_IN_ORIGINAL_TEXTS.md)
> (Surya Siddhanta, Aryabhatiya, Varahamihira, Grahalaghava in Sanskrit) confirms
> and corrects parts of this report.

The classical siddhantas never define the zodiac by a star. They define it as a computed origin, the end of Revati and start of Ashvini, where all mean planets stood together at creation. A faint star, zeta Piscium, only marks that point roughly, and the texts give star positions as polar (dhruvaka) longitudes that are accurate to a degree or two at best. The official modern Indian ayanamsa (Calendar Reform Committee 1955, Lahiri, the Indian Astronomical Ephemeris) is also not star-based. It is a numeric true ayanamsa at a fixed epoch, carried forward with a precession formula plus nutation, and it leaves Spica about 1′ short of 180°. The "True" star-anchored ayanamsas (True Chitra, True Revati, True Pushya, True Mula) are modern practitioner definitions in **ecliptic**, not polar, longitude. Their reference software, Swiss Ephemeris, computes the anchor star with **the same reductions as the planet being converted**: an apparent planet gets an apparent star, with aberration and light deflection. Moira pairs apparent planets with an astrometric anchor star. That choice is defensible, but it departs from Swiss Ephemeris by up to about ±20.5″ with a one-year period, and it breaks the engine's own invariant: Moira's apparent Spica does not come out at exactly 180° under True Chitra. **Recommendation:** make the anchor's reduction follow the planet's reduction by default (apparent planets get the apparent star, measured on the same ecliptic and equinox of date). Keep the astrometric anchor as a named, documented alternative. Keep ecliptic longitude for all "True" modes, and put any polar (dhruvaka) reading in separate, explicitly siddhantic modes.

## The siddhantas anchor a computed origin, and their stars sit in polar coordinates

### The zero point is the mean planets' common origin

The Surya Siddhanta counts a planetary revolution as "complete at the end of the asterism Revati" (SS i.27). Burgess and Whitney's 1860 translation notes that every school places the start of planetary motion at creation at that point. They add that Revati's principal star, which all authorities identify as zeta Piscium, only marks the point "most nearly": the SS puts it **10′ west** of it, while "other authorities" put it exactly on it ([Burgess 1860, pp.157–158](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). The SS gives star positions only indirectly. SS viii.1–9 assigns each yogatara a number that, multiplied by 10, gives arcminutes within its own 13°20′ segment (bhoga). From those numbers, Revati comes to **359°50′** at latitude 0, Pushya (delta Cancri) to **106°0′** at latitude 0, Chitra (Spica) to **180°0′** at 2°S, and Mula to **241°0′** at 9°S. Burgess's table confirms the Pushya and Revati figures ([Burgess 1860](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)).

None of these stars is presented as the definition of the zodiac, and the texts do not agree with each other. Burgess calls the SS value for Chitra "very erroneously defined". He notes that all other authorities except the Shakalya give Chitra **183°** of polar longitude. The line of Brahmagupta's Brahma-siddhanta (as Colebrooke reports it), the Khandakhadyaka (as al-Biruni reports it) and Bhaskara's Siddhanta Shiromani agrees with the Shiromani's longitudes, and the Grahalaghava has "a series of variations of its own". For Mula, the Grahalaghava adds 1° to give 242° polar ([Burgess 1860, pp.326, 334, 337](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). Using Spica as the defining anchor, opposite the zero point, is a later choice. Swiss Ephemeris traces it to S. B. Dikshit (1896), and the Calendar Reform Committee adopted it in 1955 ([Swiss Ephemeris §2.8.5](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)).

### Dhruvaka is a polar longitude, and the polar reading has critics

The texts give **dhruvaka** and **vikshepa**. Burgess defines dhruvaka as the point where the star's hour circle, the great circle through the *celestial* pole, meets the ecliptic, and translates it as "polar longitude". He also gives the formulas for reducing it to ecliptic coordinates ([Burgess 1860, pp.319–326](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). The Calendar Reform Committee's Appendix 5-B uses the same polar reading and the same conversion relations to date the zero point ([ICRC report, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/HistoryOfCalendarPanchangaCommittee/History-of-Calendar-Panchanga-Committee_djvu.txt)). Not everyone accepts this reading. A non-peer-reviewed essay series argues that it begins with Burgess and appears in no Indian text ([Zero Points of Vedic Astronomy](https://rajarammohanroy.medium.com/zero-points-of-vedic-astronomy-f4f8febb0b05)). Peer-reviewed reassessments in the Journal of Astrophysics and Astronomy (2019, 2020) exist but were only seen as snippets in this research ([JoAA 2019](https://www.ias.ac.in/article/fulltext/joaa/040/06/0048)). For stars near the ecliptic the choice hardly matters. zeta Psc (latitude −13′) and delta Cnc (about 0°) give nearly the same longitude either way. Spica, at about 2°S, does not. Swiss Ephemeris shows that Spica placed at *polar* 180° in 499 CE lands at **180°50′03″** ecliptic in 2000 ([Swiss Ephemeris §2.8](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)).

### Precession was a libration, and observations were good to a degree

The SS describes precession as a libration of ±27° about the fixed origin at 54″/yr. Burgess calls the passage "awkward and confused", which suggests it was added later. Bhaskara II's continuous rate (59.9″/yr) was rejected by his own commentators ([Burgess 1860, pp.244–246](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). The familiar zero-ayanamsa years are modern reconstructions, not statements in the texts. 499 CE comes from Aryabhata's age at Kali 3600. 522 CE comes from Govindasvamin's report, which Swiss Ephemeris considers probably a misreading. 285 CE was Lahiri's 1972 choice ([Swiss Ephemeris](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)). Burgess's comparison of all 28 yogataras finds an average longitude error of −56′, with individual errors from −2°4′ to +3°27′. He warns that "the accuracy of a Hindu observation is not to be relied on within a degree or two" ([Burgess 1860, pp.355–356](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). Annual aberration, at 20.5″, was not explained until Bradley in 1727 ([Wikipedia: Aberration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberration_(astronomy))), and stellar proper motion was not proven until Halley in 1718 ([Wikipedia: Proper motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_motion)). **The texts therefore cannot settle whether the anchor should be apparent or astrometric.** That is purely a modern choice of definition.

The research did not cover some sources. The Aryabhatiya and Panchasiddhantika were not checked for star catalogues. Pingree and Morrissey's identification paper ([JHA 1989](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1989JHA....20...99P/abstract)) and Pingree's precession paper (JHA 1972) were located but not read. Primary Grahalaghava and Khandakhadyaka ayanamsa rules are known here only from secondary sources.

## The official Lahiri ayanamsa is a formula and leaves Spica about 1′ short of 180°

The Calendar Reform Committee defined the initial point as "the point on the ecliptic whose true tropical longitude was 23°15′00″ as on 21 March 1956, 0h UT" (Swiss Ephemeris says 0h ET). It was carried forward with Newcomb's precession until 1984, and new mean and true algorithms were introduced afterwards ([Bhatnagar, IAU Symp. 141](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/symposium-international-astronomical-union/article/effect-of-the-new-equinox-definition-on-the-zeropoint-of-longitude-of-the-indian-calendar/93E5CF48518F9C350FB67FFC82E27187)). The committee itself was openly critical of the nirayana (sidereal) system. It froze the start of the year at a numeric tropical longitude to stop further drift, not to follow a star ([ICRC report](https://archive.org/stream/HistoryOfCalendarPanchangaCommittee/History-of-Calendar-Panchanga-Committee_djvu.txt)). The Indian Astronomical Ephemeris moved to IAU 1976 precession in 1985 and changed the epoch value to 23°15′00″.658. Swiss Ephemeris argues this is in fact a **true** (nutated) value despite its "mean" label. It reproduces IAE 2019's true ayanamsa from that value with IAU 1976 precession and IAU 1980 nutation, and PAC Kolkata confirmed the reconstruction in 2020 ([Swiss Ephemeris Appendix E](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)).

Two results follow. First, the official "true ayanamsa" means *mean ayanamsa plus nutation in longitude*. It does not mean "the star's apparent place". No anchor star enters the official computation, so aberration and light deflection have nothing to act on. Second, Spica is **not** at 180° under the official value: Swiss Ephemeris puts it at **179°58′58″–59″**. This is the gap between a formula definition and the star's proper motion and the moving ecliptic ([Swiss Ephemeris](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)). Lahiri's 1940 Panchanga Darpan value did put Spica essentially at 180° (179°59′52″ in 2000), but Lahiri later adopted the committee value "for the sake of uniformity" ([Swiss Ephemeris](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)). Which precession model current IAE editions use is still unresolved.

The practitioner schools fall into two groups. **KP and Raman are formula ayanamsas**, defined by an epoch and a rate: KP is about 291 CE and differs from Lahiri by 4–5′, and Raman follows Bhaskara's 11° in 1183 CE ([Swiss Ephemeris](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)). The **star-live** schools define the ayanamsa as the anchor star's tropical ecliptic longitude minus a fixed sidereal longitude. P. V. R. Narasimha Rao's Pushya-paksha fixes delta Cnc at 16°Cancer 0′ (106°). He chose that star because the siddhantas agree on it and its latitude is about 0°, which makes the polar-versus-ecliptic question irrelevant. His Jagannatha Hora software computes the star's tropical position at runtime ([PVR 2013](https://argala.ru/pdf-files/pp_ayanamsa.pdf)). His paper also criticises True Chitra and True Mula. For True Mula, he notes that the SS gives Mula 241° at 9°S, while lambda Sco lies near 13°S. He says nothing about aberration or proper motion. Chandra Hari's True Mula (lambda Sco at 240°) is not a literal reading of the SS either. No practitioner primary text found in this research specifies whether the anchor star should be apparent or astrometric.

## Swiss Ephemeris computes the anchor star with the planet's own flags

Swiss Ephemeris is software documentation, not an astronomical authority, but it is the de facto parity target. The C source shows how it works. `swi_get_ayanamsa_ex` adds SEFLG_NONUT and passes through only SEFLG_TRUEPOS, SEFLG_NOABERR and SEFLG_NOGDEFL from the caller. It then calls `swe_fixstar("Spica", …)` and sets the ayanamsa to `degnorm(x[0] − 180)`, with the same pattern for zeta Psc (−359°50′), delta Cnc (−106°) and lambda Sco (−240°). In the sidereal branch, the planet's **own** iflag is passed to the ayanamsa call. The star pipeline applies light deflection and annual aberration unless those flags suppress them ([sweph.c](https://github.com/aloistr/swisseph/blob/master/sweph.c)). In practice, an apparent planet is measured against the apparent star on the mean ecliptic of date. A TRUEPOS or astrometric planet is measured against the correspondingly reduced star. Heliocentric and barycentric planets are forced to a non-aberrated star. Topocentric flags are not passed on, so the star stays geocentric ([sweph.c](https://github.com/aloistr/swisseph/blob/master/sweph.c)).

The 2.05 changelog states the rule. The True ayanamsas "always keep the intended longitude, with or without… SEFLG_TRUEPOS, SEFLG_NOABERR, SEFLG_NOGDEFL". Before 2.05, Spica was exactly at 180° only when the apparent position was computed. The stated motive was that "some people may find it more natural if the star's true position is exactly at 180°" ([swephprg changelog](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swephprg.htm)). The general documentation says "not only its apparent, but also its true position", which means whichever reduction you request. The two cannot both be at 180° at once. True Chitra is explicitly defined "in ecliptic longitude (not polar!)". The polar readings exist as separate modes: 25 is Revati at polar 359°50′ in 499 CE, and 26 is Spica at polar 180° ([Swiss Ephemeris §2.8](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)). The galactic-pole modes, by contrast, always use the *true* pole position, "without aberration or light deflection" ([sweph.c](https://github.com/aloistr/swisseph/blob/master/sweph.c)). So Swiss Ephemeris's practice is pragmatic rather than doctrinal. Koch himself warns that any star-bound zodiac carries the anchor's "proper motion, its aberration, its gravitational deflection, and its parallax" into every other position, and that it rides on a moving ecliptic of date ([Swiss Ephemeris §2.8.12](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)).

Western sidereal and Babylonian systems confirm that the formula and star-live families are distinct. Fagan-Bradley is an epoch formula: 24°02′31.36″ at 1 January 1950, derived from Spica at 29°Virgo 06′05″ "(without aberration)", disregarding proper motion. Huber, Britton and the Aldebaran 15 Tau/Antares 15 Sco mode are also epoch formulas fitted to history ([Swiss Ephemeris](https://www.astro.com/swisseph/swisseph.htm)). Published SVPs apply precession and nutation to the epoch value, and the fiducial is "not marked by any star" ([Erlewine, Astro*Dictionary](https://astrologysoftware.com/community/learn/dictionary/sidereal.html)). The code confirms that only TRUE_CITRA, TRUE_REVATI, TRUE_PUSHYA, TRUE_SHEORAN, TRUE_MULA and the galactic modes are star-live ([sweph.c](https://github.com/aloistr/swisseph/blob/master/sweph.c)). **Moira's star-live "Aldebaran 15 Tau" is therefore a Moira-specific variant and not a Swiss Ephemeris mode-14 equivalent.** It should be named and documented accordingly, or a formula version should be added for parity.

Independent implementations show the same pitfall. Kerykeion's libephemeris records that its star-based ayanamsas had "up to 20.5″ error (missing aberration)" until it moved to a full apparent-place pipeline ([libephemeris precision history](https://kerykeion.net/libephemeris/docs/development/precision-history)). Jagannatha Hora computes its ayanamsa in its own module and defaults to "true" planets ([jyotish-blog 2005](https://jyotish-blog.blogspot.com/2005/06/calculations-in-jhora.html)). Its handling of aberration for the anchor star is undocumented.

## Annual aberration, not nutation, decides the apparent-versus-astrometric question

The size of the difference comes from standard astrometry. The aberration constant is 20.49552″ ([Wikipedia: Aberration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberration_(astronomy))). The usual textbook form of its effect in ecliptic longitude is Δλ ≈ −κ·cos(λ☉ − λ)/cos β. This formula was not checked against a primary almanac in this research. Annual aberration does **not** shift the whole sky equally. Subtracting an apparent Spica therefore cancels aberration only for bodies near Spica. For every other body it imposes Spica's own annual swing, about ±20.5″, on the zero point, so a body opposite Spica can be off by up to about 41″ in differential terms. Nutation in longitude is different: it shifts every ecliptic longitude equally, so it cancels exactly as long as star and planet are on the **same** equinox. That is a check worth running in Moira. If the astrometric star is placed on the mean equinox of date while the apparent planets are on the true equinox, a stray Δψ of up to about 17″ enters every sidereal longitude. Light deflection matters only near the Sun, when Spica is in conjunction around mid-October. Spica's proper motion moves it only about 1′ in 1,500 years. The deflection and proper-motion figures come from background knowledge and still need checking against SIMBAD or the Explanatory Supplement.

That leaves three candidate definitions, each with a different meaning. With an **apparent anchor matched to apparent planets** (the Swiss Ephemeris default), a sidereal longitude is an observable quantity: the apparent elongation from the star, offset by its nominal value. Spica computed through the engine always comes out at exactly 180°, and a planet in apparent conjunction with Spica reads 180°. The cost is that the zodiac's zero point wobbles annually by about ±20″. With an **astrometric anchor and apparent planets** (Moira today), the frame is fixed to the star's geometric direction, which matches Koch's "more natural" rationale. But it mixes reductions: Moira's own apparent Spica will read 180° ± about 20″, and the output disagrees with Swiss Ephemeris's default by the same amount. A **smooth formula** (epoch plus precession) is a clean definition, but it is the official Lahiri or Fagan-Bradley family, not a "True" one. It lets the star drift by proper motion and planetary precession, which is exactly what the star-live modes were created to avoid.

## Conclusion

Every layer of tradition except the modern True modes is indifferent to an apparent anchor. The siddhantas worked to a degree, and the official Indian and Western sidereal definitions are formulas with no star. The only real constraints are internal consistency and parity, and both favour making the star's reduction follow the planet's. For Moira, the most robust design is to compute the anchor star through **the same pipeline as the planet being converted**. Apparent geocentric planets get the apparent star, with proper motion, parallax, light deflection and annual aberration applied, on the same ecliptic and equinox of date. True or astrometric planets get the matching star. Heliocentric output gets a non-aberrated star, and the geocentric star is kept for topocentric output. This restores the invariant "True Chitra puts Moira's own Spica at 180°" and matches Swiss Ephemeris 2.05+ to sub-arcsecond level. The current astrometric anchor should stay as an explicitly named alternative (a "star-fixed frame" option), not be silently replaced. Moira should state whether its reported ayanamsa includes Δψ, and add a parity test that runs across a full year so the ±20″ aberration signature becomes visible.

On geometry, keep **ecliptic longitude** for every "True" mode. That is how Swiss Ephemeris, PVR and the Chandra Hari line all define them, and the classical polar figures do not match those numbers anyway: SS Spica 180° polar is about 180°50′ ecliptic, and SS Mula is 241°, not 240°. A dhruvaka-faithful option is a different ayanamsa, not a correction of these ones. If Moira wants it, it belongs in separate, epoch-stated modes such as Swiss Ephemeris's polar 499 CE variants, labelled as Burgess-style siddhantic reconstructions. Finally, Moira's documentation should state that every star-anchored ayanamsa is a **modern interpretation**. The texts anchor the zodiac to a computed origin, disagree among themselves by up to 3° on Chitra, and are open to a contested reading of what dhruvaka means.
