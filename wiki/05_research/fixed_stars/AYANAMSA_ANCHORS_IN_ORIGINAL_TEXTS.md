# The texts anchor segments, not stars

*Addendum to "Match the anchor star to the planets" (Star-anchored ayanamsa doctrine)*

Read in the original Sanskrit, none of the five classical sources defines the sidereal zodiac by a star. The Surya Siddhanta's zero is **"pauṣṇānte"**, the end of the Revatī *segment*. Its own catalogue puts the Revatī junction star **10′ before** that point, at 359°50′. The Aryabhatiya counts from Meṣa and gives no star positions at all. Varāhamihira gives only a partial junction-star list and a solstice shift, with no rate. The Grahalaghava's ayanamsa is pure arithmetic: **(Śaka − 444) ÷ 60 degrees**, zero in 522 CE, growing 1′ a year. That last point is now confirmed from the verse itself rather than from secondary sources.

The independent reading confirms most of the earlier report and sharpens three points:

1. **The polar (dhruvaka) reading of star coordinates is in the texts, not invented by Burgess.** Gaṇeśa's verse says outright that his dhruvakas already carry the *āyana-dṛkkarma* correction. Two commentaries written around 1600 describe how to observe them with a ring through the celestial pole.
2. **The Surya Siddhanta's "±27° libration" sits partly in the commentary.** The verse gives 600 cycles per yuga and a doḥ × 3/10 rule. The back-and-forth reading comes from that operation and from the commentators, and a second reading of "30 per yuga" was already circulating by Bhāskara II's time.
3. **Every modern "True" anchor is a modern convention in ecliptic longitude.** That covers True Chitra at 180°, True Mula at 240°, and the ecliptic versions of True Revati and True Pushya. Pushya at 106° with latitude 0 is the only anchor value on which the Surya Siddhanta and Grahalaghava agree outright, and both give it in polar coordinates.

For Moira, the textual material supports adding text-faithful **arithmetic** modes (Grahalaghava; optionally the Surya Siddhanta trepidation rule) and separate **polar, epoch-stated** siddhantic modes. Keep the existing "True" modes as clearly labelled modern ecliptic definitions.

## How this addendum separates text, commentary and interpretation

Every claim below carries one of three layers:

- **Text**: the verse as printed in a critical or traditional edition.
- **Commentary**: the traditional Sanskrit commentators, Ranganātha (1603) on the Surya Siddhanta, Parameśvara on the Aryabhatiya, and Mallāri and Viśvanātha on the Grahalaghava.
- **Modern interpretation**: Burgess, Thibaut, Clark, Neugebauer & Pingree, Rao & Uma, Dikshit, Swiss Ephemeris, and my own arithmetic.

**Translations marked "my" are fresh readings of the Sanskrit**, made in this research from these sources:

- **Surya Siddhanta:** Michio Yano's GRETIL e-text, collated from three editions ([GRETIL](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_sUryasiddhAnta.txt)), checked line by line against the Rashtriya Sanskrit Sansthan edition with Ranganātha's commentary ([RSS](https://archive.org/download/suryasiddhantasanskrittextrashtriyasanskritsansthan_581_F/Suryasiddhanta%20Sanskrit%20Text%20-%20Rashtriya%20Sanskrit%20Sansthan_djvu.txt)).
- **Aryabhatiya:** Kern's 1874 edition, read from page images ([Kern](https://archive.org/details/aryabhatiyawithc00arya)).
- **Pañcasiddhāntikā:** Thibaut & Dvivedi's 1889 edition, read from page images ([Thibaut](https://archive.org/details/wg1078)).
- **Bṛhat Saṃhitā:** the GRETIL text ([GRETIL BS](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/plaintext/sa_varAhamihira-bRhatsaMhitA.txt)).
- **Grahalaghava:** the Motilal edition with Mallāri, Viśvanātha and Kedāradatta Jośī ([Jośī ed.](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnasanskritbhashyamallariviswanathahindied.kedardattajoshimlbd_626_p)).

The Surya Siddhanta also had a genuinely independent second translator: Bāpu Deva Śāstrī (1861), who worked from Ranganātha separately from Burgess ([Bapu Deva](https://archive.org/download/in.ernet.dli.2015.46927/2015.46927.Translation-Of-The-Surya-Siddhanta_djvu.txt)).

Two scholarly translations named in the brief were **not** consulted: Shukla & Sarma's 1976 Aryabhatiya, and Shukla's 1957 Surya Siddhanta with Parameśvara's commentary. Where they matter, the gap is noted.

## The Surya Siddhanta puts zero at a segment's end and its stars in polar coordinates

### i.27 and i.57: the zero is "the end of Pauṣṇa", and the planets meet at the end of the Kṛta age

> *teṣāṃ tu parivartena pauṣṇānte bhagaṇaḥ smṛtaḥ* (i.27cd)

- **Gloss:** *parivartena* "by the full circuit"; *pauṣṇa-ante* "at the end of Pauṣṇa". Pauṣṇa is the asterism whose deity is Pūṣan, i.e. Revatī. *bhagaṇaḥ smṛtaḥ* "a revolution is reckoned".
- **My translation:** "A revolution is reckoned by their completing the circuit at the end of Pauṣṇa (Revatī)."
- **Comparison:** Burgess has "the revolution is accounted complete at the end of the asterism Revati", which matches ([Burgess p.157](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). Bāpu Deva adds "ζ Piscium" in parentheses, which is his gloss, not the text's.
- **What the verse does and does not say:** it names a segment boundary, not a star.
- **Commentary:** Ranganātha places the zero at "the place just ahead of the Revatī junction star" (*revatīyogatārāsannāgrimasthānam*). His reason is that at creation Brahmā set all the planets on the ecliptic near that star ([RSS p.16](https://archive.org/download/suryasiddhantasanskrittextrashtriyasanskritsansthan_581_F/Suryasiddhanta%20Sanskrit%20Text%20-%20Rashtriya%20Sanskrit%20Sansthan_djvu.txt)).

> *asmin kṛtayugasyānte sarve madhyagatā grahāḥ / vinā tu pātamandoccān meṣādau tulyatām itāḥ* (i.57)

- **My translation:** "At the end of this Kṛta-yuga all the planets in mean motion, except the nodes and apsides, came together at the beginning of Aries."
- **Comparison:** Burgess agrees ([Burgess pp.180–181](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)).
- **Refinement to the earlier report:** the earlier report said the mean planets "stood together at creation". The only explicit conjunction verse in the text refers to **the end of the Kṛta-yuga**, and it excludes apsides and nodes. A conjunction at creation follows from the whole-revolution bhagaṇa method, and Ranganātha states it, but no verse in i.24–58 says it.

### iii.9–12: six hundred swings, a doḥ × 3/10 rule, and a check against the shadow

> *triṃśatkṛtyo yuge bhānāṃ cakraṃ prāk parilambate* (iii.9ab)
> *taddos trighnā daśāptāṃśā vijñeyā ayanābhidhāḥ* (iii.10ab)

- **Gloss:**
  - *triṃśat-kṛtyaḥ* is "thirty *kṛti*s". *Kṛti* is the word-numeral for 20 (from a 20-syllable metre), so this is 600.
  - *bhānāṃ cakram* "the circle of the asterisms"; *prāk parilambate* "hangs back eastward". This is the same verb *lamb* that i.25 uses for the planets' lag.
  - *tad-doḥ* "its doḥ", the arc reduced to the 0–90° quadrant; *tri-ghnāḥ daśa-āpta* "multiplied by three, divided by ten"; *ayana-abhidhāḥ* "called ayana [degrees]".
- **My translation:** "Six hundred times in a Mahāyuga the circle of the asterisms swings back eastward… take its doḥ, multiply by three and divide by ten: the result in degrees is called the *ayana*."
- **Comparison:** Burgess's "thirty score of revolutions" and "degrees called those of the precession" match the arithmetic ([Burgess p.243](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). Bāpu Deva has "librates 600 times", which already takes Ranganātha's view.

What the text supplies is a cycle count (600 per yuga, i.e. a 7,200-year period) and an operation whose maximum is 90° × 3/10 = **27°**. The **libration** reading rests on that doḥ operation and on *āvṛtya* ("turning back") in v.12. Ranganātha makes the libration explicit:

- **The 27° swing:** the star-circle moves 27° west, returns, moves 27° east and returns.
- **The 30-per-yuga reading is an error:** he calls the reading *triṃśat-kṛtvaḥ* ("thirty times") mistaken (*prāmādika*), citing the Soma-siddhānta.
- **No full revolution:** *parilambate* means the circle never completes a revolution.

([RSS pp.61–62](https://archive.org/download/suryasiddhantasanskrittextrashtriyasanskritsansthan_581_F/Suryasiddhanta%20Sanskrit%20Text%20-%20Rashtriya%20Sanskrit%20Sansthan_djvu.txt))

Burgess reads the evidence differently:

- **Bhāskara II reported 30:** Bhāskara II read the Surya Siddhanta as giving **thirty** revolutions per yuga.
- **"Thirty twenties" is unusual:** the expression has no parallel elsewhere in the text.
- **Verse 9 alone implies revolution:** taken by itself, verse 9 suggests full revolution rather than libration.

([Burgess pp.244–245](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt))

**So the variant is real and old.** "±27°, 54″/yr" is the Ranganātha-school reading of a verse that also circulated with "30".

> *prāk cakraṃ calitaṃ hīne chāyārkāt karaṇāgate* (iii.11cd)

- **My translation:** "When the computed Sun is less than the shadow-Sun, the circle has moved eastward [by the difference]."
- **Comparison:** Burgess makes "the circle, as thus corrected" the subject of the preceding *sphuṭaṃ dṛktulyatāṃ gacchet*. Ranganātha takes *sphuṭam* as an adverb meaning "plainly". The grammar more naturally makes the neuter compound *krānti-chāyā-caradala-ādikam* ("declination, shadow, ascensional difference etc.") the subject: those quantities "become correct" at the solstices and equinoxes.
- **What all readings agree on:** the ayanamsa is measured as **computed sidereal Sun minus observed tropical Sun**, and no star enters the measurement. Burgess's worked value for 1 January 1860, **20°24′38.67″**, is a usable test vector ([Burgess p.245](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). He also notes that the literal rule puts the displacement in the wrong direction unless the observational test in v.11–12 fixes the sign.

### viii.1–9: a fully determinate catalogue in a 10× code

> *svabhogo 'tha daśāhataḥ / bhavanty atītadhiṣṇyānāṃ bhogaliptāyutā dhruvāḥ* (viii.1bcd)

- **My translation:** "Each one's own figure multiplied by ten, increased by the segment-minutes (800′ each, ii.64) of the asterisms already passed, gives the dhruvas."
- **Decoding:** the coded words are read right to left:
  - *aṣṭārṇavāḥ* = 48 → Aśvinī 8°0′
  - *aṅgāgāḥ* = 76 → Puṣya 106°0′
  - *khavedāḥ* = 40 → Citrā 180°0′
  - *rasāḥ* = 6 → Mūla 241°0′
  - *randhrādrayaḥ* = 79 → Revatī 359°50′
- **Latitudes:** they are measured *svād apakramāt*, "from its own declination-point" (viii.6). Aśvinī is 10°N, Puṣya 0, Citrā 2°S, Mūla 9°S, Revatī 0.
- **Agreement:** my decoding agrees exactly with Burgess's table, with Ranganātha's worked examples, and with Bāpu Deva's list, apart from two OCR slips in the last ([Burgess pp.319–320](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). The numbers do not depend on the translator.
- **The only textual variant found:** Ārdrā (4, against 49 or 39), which Ranganātha rejects.
- **Which star is the junction star (viii.16–19):** Aśvinī's is the **northern** star, Puṣya's the **middle**, Revatī's the **southern**, Mūla's the **eastern**. Citrā falls under "the bright one" ([Burgess p.361](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). Burgess suspects these verses are later additions.

### viii.12: the armillary instruction, and where the polar coordinate comes from

> *golaṃ labdhvā parīkṣeta vikṣepaṃ dhruvakaṃ sphuṭam* (viii.12cd; RSS reads *baddhvā*)

- **My translation:** "Having built a sphere, one should check the vikṣepa and dhruvaka exactly."
- **Comparison:** Burgess has "corrected (sphuta) latitude and polar longitude", Bāpu Deva "apparent latitudes and longitudes".
- **What the verse supports:** taken alone, either reading. Ranganātha's commentary is decisive about the tradition's understanding:
  - **The vikṣepa** is *dhruvābhimukha*, "directed toward the [celestial] pole".
  - **The dhruvaka** is *āyana-dṛkkarma-saṃskṛta*, i.e. it already contains the correction for the ecliptic's tilt to the equator.
  - **The observing procedure:** mount a ring on the polar axis. Sight the Revatī star and set on it the ecliptic point **10′ west of the end of Pisces**. Then read each junction star's dhruva where the ring cuts the ecliptic.
  - **Why not ecliptic coordinates:** he rejects them because there are no stars at the ecliptic poles to align the ring with.
  - **The values are epoch-bound:** he says the values are "unstable because of the ayanāṃśa", were stated as fixed only for the author's time, and **must be re-observed** in other periods.

  ([RSS p.126](https://archive.org/download/suryasiddhantasanskrittextrashtriyasanskritsansthan_581_F/Suryasiddhanta%20Sanskrit%20Text%20-%20Rashtriya%20Sanskrit%20Sansthan_djvu.txt))
- **The second translator agrees:** Bāpu Deva independently defines "apparent longitude" as the ecliptic intercept of the star's circle of declination ([Bapu Deva](https://archive.org/download/in.ernet.dli.2015.46927/2015.46927.Translation-Of-The-Surya-Siddhanta_djvu.txt)).
- **Modern interpretation:** Burgess argued the armillary reading was "forced upon the passage by the commentators" ([Burgess pp.357–359](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). He disputes the *purpose* of the verse, not the meaning of the coordinate.
- **A slip in Burgess:** on p.358 he paraphrases Ranganātha's ecliptic point as 10′ **east** of the end of Pisces. The Sanskrit, *mīnāntād daśakalāntaritapaścādbhāgam*, says **behind (west)**. That matches 359°50′ and Burgess's own p.158.

### What conversion to ecliptic coordinates does to these values

Converting the polar values to ecliptic coordinates with the Surya Siddhanta's own 24° obliquity (my calculation) gives:

| Star | Polar (dhruvaka) | Ecliptic longitude | Ecliptic latitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citrā | 180°0′ | **180°49′** | 1°50′S |
| Mūla | 241°0′ | 242°55′ | 8°48′S |
| Aśvinī | 8°0′ | 12°04′ | 9°09′N |
| Puṣya | 106°0′ | 106°0′ | 0° |
| Revatī | 359°50′ | 359°50′ | 0° |

Burgess's own reductions are within 5–20′ of these figures, for example Citrā 180°48′/1°50′S ([Burgess pp.327–337](https://archive.org/download/jstor-592174/592174_djvu.txt)). The stars with zero latitude in the table (Puṣya and Revatī) convert unchanged.

## The Aryabhatiya counts from Meṣa, names no star, and moves no equinox

> *budhāhny ajārkodayāc ca laṅkāyām* (Gītikā 2d)

- **Gloss:** *budha-ahni* "on Wednesday"; *aja* "the Ram" (Meṣa); *arka-udayāt* "from sunrise"; *laṅkāyām* "at Laṅkā".
- **My translation:** "[The revolutions are counted] from Aries, from sunrise on Wednesday at Laṅkā."
- **Comparison:** Clark has "starting at the beginning of Mesa at sunrise on Wednesday at Lanka". He supplies "beginning of", which is clearly implied ([Clark p.9](https://archive.org/details/TheAryabhatiyaOfAryabhata)).
- **Commentary:** Parameśvara ties the moment to **the start of the Kṛtayuga** (*kṛtayugādau*) ([Kern p.7](https://archive.org/details/aryabhatiyawithc00arya/page/n20)).

The revolution numbers decode exactly from the alphabetic code. For example, *khyughṛ* gives 4,320,000 solar revolutions and *ṅiśibuṇḷṣkhṛ* gives 1,582,237,500 rotations. Rotations are counted against the asterisms, so the year comes out sidereal (365.25868 days).

> *meṣādeḥ kanyāntaṃ samam udag apamaṇḍalārdham apayātam* (Gola 1ab)

- **My translation:** "From the beginning of Meṣa to the end of Kanyā, half the ecliptic deviates northward."
- **What this means:** the text's only geometric definition of Meṣa 0° is **the vernal equinox** ([Clark p.63](https://archive.org/details/TheAryabhatiyaOfAryabhata)). Āryabhaṭa gives no rule separating it from the sidereal origin, and he calls the asterisms stationary (*acalāni bhāni*, IV.9).
- **No star catalogue:** a search of Clark's complete translation finds none. Every "asterism" is generic.
- **Kali 3600:** the famous date enters through III.10, *ṣaṣṭy-abdānāṃ ṣaṣṭir yadā vyatītās trayaś ca yugapādāḥ*. That verse gives the author's **age** (23) at Kali 3600 ([Kern p.58](https://archive.org/details/aryabhatiyawithc00arya/page/n69)), not an equinox event.

Trepidation appears in Kern's edition only in **commentary**. On III.10 Parameśvara quotes an "ayana-correction":

> *kalyabdāt khakhaṣaṭkṛtihīnād vasuśūnyanāgaśarabhaktāt*

- **What the verse prescribes:** from the Kali year subtract 3,600, then divide by 5,808. Each quadrant is 1,452 years. Bhuja-years × 2 ÷ 121 gives degrees.
- **What that implies:** an oscillation of **±24° with a 5,808-year period**, zero at Kali 3600 (499 CE), about 59.5″/yr ([Kern p.59](https://archive.org/details/aryabhatiyawithc00arya/page/n70)).
- **Not in the core text:** Clark notes that the line Colebrooke attributed to Āryabhaṭa, "the circle goes 24° both ways", is **not found** in the Aryabhatiya ([Clark p.xv](https://archive.org/details/TheAryabhatiyaOfAryabhata)).

The 499 CE "zero ayanamsa" is therefore a reasonable reconstruction from III.10 plus the commentarial rule. It is not something Āryabhaṭa wrote.

## Varāhamihira records an observed solstice shift but gives no rate

> *āśleṣārdhād dakṣiṇam uttaram ayanaṃ raver dhaniṣṭhādyam / nūnaṃ kadācid āsīd yenoktaṃ pūrvaśāstreṣu* (BS 3.1)
> *sāmpratam ayanaṃ savituḥ karkaṭakādyaṃ mṛgāditaś cānyat / uktābhāvo vikṛtiḥ pratyakṣaparīkṣaṇair vyaktiḥ* (BS 3.2)

- **Gloss:**
  - *āśleṣā-ardhāt* "from the half (middle) of Āśleṣā"; *dhaniṣṭhā-ādyam* "beginning at Dhaniṣṭhā"; *pūrva-śāstreṣu* "in former treatises".
  - *sāmpratam* "now"; *karkaṭaka-ādyam* "beginning at Cancer"; *mṛga-āditaḥ* "from the start of Capricorn".
  - *vikṛtiḥ* "alteration, anomaly"; *pratyakṣa-parīkṣaṇaiḥ* "by tests of direct observation".
- **My translation:** "The southern course of the Sun from the middle of Āśleṣā and the northern from Dhaniṣṭhā must surely have held once, since the former treatises say so. Now one course begins at Karkaṭa and the other at Makara. The failure of the stated [position] is a change, made evident by direct observation."
- **Comparison:** Chidambaram Iyer (1884) and Subrahmanya Sastri (1946) agree in substance ([Iyer](https://archive.org/details/bihatsahitvarah00iyergoog); [Sastri](https://archive.org/details/varahamihira-brihat-samhita-panditabhushana-v-subrahmanya-sastri-1946-with-english-ocr-layer)). Sastri renders *ardha* as "latter half" rather than "middle", which moves the implied old solstice by up to 6°40′.
- **Method:** BS 3.3 names the checks: a distant horizon mark at sunrise or sunset, or the shadow's entry and exit in a large circle.
- **What the passage leaves out:** it states no rate, no mechanism and no direction. Verses 3.4–5 treat a short solstice as an omen.

> *āśleṣārdhād āsīd yadā nivṛttiḥ kiloṣṇakiraṇasya / yuktam ayanaṃ tadāsīt sāmpratam ayanaṃ punarvasutaḥ* (PS III.21)

- **My translation:** "When, as is said, the Sun's turning was from mid-Āśleṣā, the ayana was then *yukta*. Now the ayana is from Punarvasu."
- **The translators split on *yukta*:**
  - **Thibaut** reads "right" (correct) ([Thibaut p.18](https://archive.org/details/wg1078)).
  - **Neugebauer & Pingree** read it technically, as the ayana-correction having been **positive**. They bracket the positions as 113;20° and 90°, which is their interpretation, not the Sanskrit ([N–P p.45](https://archive.org/details/pancasiddhantika1_1970)).
- **The displacement:** III.22 ties the old offset to the Sun's greatest declination (*kāṣṭhā*, which N–P gloss as 23;20°). Mid-Āśleṣā (113°20′) minus 90° is exactly **23°20′**.
- **Varāhamihira's frame:** III.23 puts the equinox at "the beginning of Meṣa and Tulā", and III.25 puts the solstices at Makara and Karkaṭa. So **at Varāhamihira's epoch (c. 505 CE) the zodiac's zero coincides with the equinox**, a frame statement with no star attached.
- **Thibaut's caution:** Varāhamihira "certainly does not render it clear anywhere" that the shift is a gradually accumulating process ([Thibaut pp.L–LI](https://archive.org/details/wg1078)).

The Pañcasiddhāntikā does give a **partial yogatārā list** (XIV.34–37). Each star is placed in degrees within its own nakṣatra and in *hastas* of latitude. It covers Kṛttikā, Rohiṇī, Punarvasu, Puṣya, Āśleṣā, Maghā and Citrā, and **has no Revatī, Aśvinī or Mūla**.

> *citrārdhāṣṭamabhāge dakṣiṇataḥ saṃsthite tribhir hastaiḥ* (XIV.37ab)

- **My translation:** "Citrā's [star] in the 'half-eighth' degree, i.e. at 7½°, lies 3 hastas south."
- **Puṣya (XIV.35d):** *puṣyasyodak caturthe 'ṃśe*, "Puṣya's at the 4th degree, north".
- **Translators:** Thibaut reads Puṣya's latitude as 3½ hastas north ([Thibaut pp.82–83](https://archive.org/details/wg1078)). N–P's OCR shows 4½ for Puṣya and different fractions for Rohiṇī; this still needs checking against their page image.
- **Absolute positions (my arithmetic, counting from Aśvinī 0° as Thibaut directs):** **Citrā ≈ 180°50′** and **Puṣya ≈ 97°20′**.
- **Latitudes:** with Thibaut's scale (Moon's 34′ = 15 aṅgulas, 24 aṅgulas to a hasta), a hasta is about 54′. Citrā's latitude is then about 2°43′S and Puṣya's about 3°10′N.
- **Unstated coordinate type:** the text does not say whether these are polar or ecliptic.
- **The zodiac's division:** Bṛhat Saṃhitā ch. 101 fixes the signs to nakṣatra quarters, *aśvinyo 'tha bharaṇyo bahulāpādaś ca kīrtyate meṣaḥ* ("Aśvinī, Bharaṇī and a quarter of Kṛttikā are Meṣa"). That is a segment definition, again with no star.

## The Grahalaghava makes the ayanamsa pure arithmetic: zero in 522 CE, one arcminute a year

> *atha vedābdhyabdhyūnaḥ kharasahṛtaḥ śako 'yanāṃśāḥ* (ch. 2, v. 7d)

- **Gloss:** *veda-abdhi-abdhi-ūnaḥ* "diminished by 4-4-4", read right to left as 444; *kha-rasa-hṛtaḥ* "divided by 0-6", i.e. 60; *śakaḥ* "the Śaka year"; *ayanāṃśāḥ* "the ayana-degrees".
- **My translation:** "Now: the Śaka year less 444, divided by 60, gives the ayanāṃśa [in degrees]."
- **Comparison:**
  - **Jośī's Hindi** says the same ([Jośī ed. p.67](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnasanskritbhashyamallariviswanathahindied.kedardattajoshimlbd_626_p)).
  - **Rao & Uma** do not render the clause at their Śloka 7 heading. They apply it elsewhere, e.g. Śaka 1920 → 24°36′, which they set against the Indian Ephemeris's 23°50′08″ for 1998 ([Rao & Uma p.S123](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnaed.balachandraraos.umas.k.ijhssuppliment_545_)).
- **The zero year is confirmed from the verse:** zero at **Śaka 444 = 522 CE**, a linear rate of **60″/yr**, no oscillation, and **no star named**.
- **Test values:**

  | Śaka year | Ayanamsa | Source |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1442 (1520 CE, the work's epoch) | 16°38′ | arithmetic |
  | 1534 | **18°10′** | Viśvanātha's worked example ([Jośī ed. p.65](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnasanskritbhashyamallariviswanathahindied.kedardattajoshimlbd_626_p)) |
  | 1809 | **22°45′** | Dikshit ([Dikshit p.291](https://archive.org/details/bharatiya-jyotish-sastra-2-sankar-balakrishna-dikshit)) |

Mallāri's commentary does two things:

- **It derives the ayanamsa from observation:** the tropical Sun from the noon altitude, minus the computed Sun.
- **It attaches a trepidation gloss:** reversal after 1,440 years, with a maximum of 24° at 1′/yr or 27° at 54″/yr. This reconciles Gaṇeśa with the Surya Siddhanta ([Jośī ed. p.65](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnasanskritbhashyamallariviswanathahindied.kedardattajoshimlbd_626_p)).

The verse itself is linear and unbounded.

The star list in ch. 11 is the most important finding for Moira:

> *khaṃ dattāyanadṛkkriyāḥ syur iha* (ch. 11, v. 2c)

- **My translation:** "[Revatī's is] zero. Here [the dhruvakas] have had the āyana-dṛkkarma applied."
- **What this establishes:** **the author himself, in the verse, says his star longitudes are polar**. That does not depend on any commentator.
- **Comparison:** Rao & Uma translate the term as "(polar longitudes)" ([Rao & Uma p.S303](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnaed.balachandraraos.umas.k.ijhssuppliment_545_)).
- **Commentary:** Mallāri describes the same pole-axis armillary as Ranganātha: sight the Revatī star and mark *mīnānte*, "at the end of Pisces", on the ecliptic ([Jośī ed. p.286](https://archive.org/details/grahalaghavaganesadaivajnasanskritbhashyamallariviswanathahindied.kedardattajoshimlbd_626_p)).
- **The decoded values:**
  - *dasrād aṣṭa*: Aśvinī 8
  - *rasadiśaḥ*: Puṣya 106
  - *tryaṣṭābjāḥ*: Citrā **183**
  - *dvijināḥ*: Mūla **242**
  - *kham*: **Revatī 0**
- **The latitudes (v. 3):** Aśvinī 10°N, Puṣya 0, Citrā 2°S, **Mūla 8°S**, Revatī 0.
- **Unresolved variant readings** (none affects the five anchor stars):
  - **Hasta:** metre favours 170; both published translations give 160.
  - **Punarvasu latitude:** the print reads 9 (*aṅka*), Rao & Uma give 6.
  - **Dhaniṣṭhā and Śatabhiṣaj latitudes:** my parse gives 36 and 0, which fits the sky; Rao & Uma's 6 and 3 does not.

**Check against modern star positions (my calculation).** The research precessed J2000 star places to 522 CE with Lieske's formulae. Gaṇeśa's dhruvakas then match actual polar longitudes to within about 2°: ζ Psc 359.4°, β Ari 9.8°, δ Cnc 108.1°, α Vir 182.4°, λ Sco 241.1°. By 1520, though, the 60″/yr rule has run about **2.3° ahead** of true precession. Under Gaṇeśa's own ayanamsa ζ Psc sits near 356.6° while Spica sits near **180.5°**.

That bears out Dikshit's observation that Spica at 180° tracks the Grahalaghava ([Dikshit pp.185, 325](https://archive.org/details/bharatiya-jyotish-sastra-2-sankar-balakrishna-dikshit)). It is **a coincidence of rate and epoch, valid within about ±1° only from roughly 1300 to 2100 CE**, not a definition in the text.

## Five junction stars across five texts

The table sets out the textual values. Polar means dhruvaka and vikṣepa; "nakṣatra-degree" means a position counted within the asterism's 13°20′ segment. Pañcasiddhāntikā absolute longitudes are my arithmetic on Thibaut's reading. "—" means the text gives no value.

| Star (usual ID) | Surya Siddhanta (text) | Aryabhatiya | Pañcasiddhāntikā XIV | Bṛhat Saṃhitā | Grahalaghava (text) | Modern "True" mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Revatī** (ζ Psc) | 359°50′ polar, 0°; zero point 10′ *east* of it | — | — (absent) | — | **0° polar, 0°** | 359°50′ ecliptic |
| **Aśvinī** (β Ari) | 8°0′ polar, 10°N (northern star) | — | — | — | 8° polar, 10°N | none |
| **Puṣya** (δ Cnc) | **106°0′ polar, 0°** (middle star) | — | 4th degree of Puṣya ≈ 97°20′, ~3½ hastas N | — | **106° polar, 0°** | 106° ecliptic |
| **Citrā** (Spica) | **180°0′ polar**, 2°S (≈180°49′ ecliptic) | — | 7½° in Citrā ≈ **180°50′**, 3 hastas S | Apāṃvatsa due north of Citrā (25.4) | **183° polar**, 2°S | 180° ecliptic |
| **Mūla** (λ Sco) | **241° polar, 9°S** (eastern star) | — | — | — | **242° polar, 8°S** | 240° ecliptic |

Three patterns stand out:

1. **Puṣya at 106° with latitude 0 is the only anchor value on which both full catalogues agree.** It is also the only one where polar and ecliptic coordinates coincide. Narasimha Rao's choice of Pushya on exactly these grounds holds up against the primary texts.
2. **No source gives Citrā 180° in ecliptic longitude, and no source gives Mūla 240°.** Those are modern values.
3. **Varāhamihira's Pañcasiddhāntikā has an odd coincidence.** Its Citrā at about 180°50′ almost equals the Surya Siddhanta's polar 180° converted to ecliptic (180°49′). If the Pañcasiddhāntikā's figures are ecliptic, the two texts may preserve the same observation in different coordinates. Its Puṣya at about 97° is roughly 9° short of every other value, and the notes do not explain why: it could be a different star, a different reading, or a different frame.

## What the original-text reading confirms, corrects and leaves open

Several questions remain open:

- **Older commentary unchecked.** Parameśvara's Surya Siddhanta commentary (c. 1432, in Shukla 1957) was not read. It would show whether the "600" reading and the polar/re-observation doctrine predate Ranganātha.
- **Pañcasiddhāntikā coordinates.** Their type (polar or ecliptic), the N–P fractions for Rohiṇī and Puṣya, and the anomalous Puṣya are unresolved.
- **Unchecked translations.** Shukla & Sarma's Aryabhatiya readings were not checked.
- **Year convention.** The Grahalaghava's ±1-year ambiguity remains: Mallāri glosses "current" Śaka year (*vartamāna*).
- **Star identifications.** The modern identifications (β Ari, δ Cnc, λ Sco) rest on Burgess and the commentators. Burgess himself marks λ Sco as only probable.

## What this means for Moira

**Which anchors are textual.** "Textual" means stated in a verse; the rest are modern definitions.

- **Textual:**
  - the end of the Revatī segment as zero;
  - ζ Psc at 359°50′ polar (SS) or 0° polar (GL);
  - δ Cnc at 106° polar, latitude 0, in both SS and GL;
  - Spica at 180° polar (SS) or 183° polar (GL);
  - λ Sco at 241° polar, 9°S (SS) or 242°, 8°S (GL).
- **Modern:** True Chitra at 180° ecliptic, True Mula at 240° ecliptic, and the "True" Revati and Pushya modes as ecliptic definitions.
- **Close to textual:** True Revati and True Pushya survive the translation from polar to ecliptic with negligible error, because both stars lie near the ecliptic.

Moira's documentation should therefore label every "True" mode as a **modern ecliptic-longitude convention inspired by the siddhantic catalogue**. True Chitra and True Mula in particular should not be described as "the Surya Siddhanta's anchor". The earlier recommendation is unchanged: compute the anchor star through the same reductions as the planet. No text bears on it.

**Offer a Grahalaghava mode, defined arithmetically.** The rule is short, explicit and verse-attested, which makes it the cleanest text-faithful ayanamsa in the corpus. It should be:

- **Defined as** ayanamsa = (Śaka − 444)/60°, which is zero in 522 CE and grows 60″/yr. It is **a formula mode, not a star mode**.
- **Configured for two choices:** whether the year is current or elapsed (the commentary favours current), and whether to step by whole years as the text does or interpolate as a documented smoothing.
- **Tested against** Śaka 1534 → 18°10′, Śaka 1809 → 22°45′ and Śaka 1920 → 24°36′.
- **Documented as historical.** It overshoots true precession by about 9.8″/yr; modern Grahalaghava pañcāṅgas abandoned it, and Jośī calls it very crude.

**Offer a Surya Siddhanta trepidation mode only as a reconstruction.** If Moira offers one, it must state its choices, because the verse leaves them open:

- **the 600-cycle reading**, as against the 30-cycle reading;
- **the sign convention**;
- **the epoch phase**.

Its test value is Burgess's 20°24′38.67″ for 1 January 1860. The commentarial Āryabhaṭa rule (±24°, 5,808 years, zero at Kali 3600) can be added under the commentator's name, never Āryabhaṭa's.

**Keep polar and ecliptic modes strictly separate.** The polar coordinate is genuinely textual, so a dhruvaka-faithful mode is a legitimate addition. It defines a *different* ayanamsa, though; it does not correct the True modes. Such a mode should:

- **name its star, value and epoch**, in the manner of Swiss Ephemeris's "polar longitude in 499 CE" variants;
- **convert through the hour circle**;
- **document its obliquity:** use the texts' 24° obliquity when reproducing the internal frame, or the obliquity of date otherwise.

Ranganātha's own doctrine adds a third possibility: a star held at a fixed *polar* longitude **of date**, re-observed as the coordinate drifts. If implemented, it needs its own name.

## Conclusion

The independent reading moves the debate from "which star did the ancients use?" to "which layer of the tradition is being reproduced?". The verses define segments, mean-motion epochs and arithmetic corrections. The commentators define observing procedures that make the Revatī star the operational reference, in polar coordinates, valid only for their own time. Modern practice defines star-live ecliptic anchors. All three are legitimate, but they are three distinct objects, and an engine that names its modes by layer avoids the commonest error in this field: presenting a modern ecliptic convention as an ancient textual fact.

The most consequential new fact is that Gaṇeśa states his star longitudes are polar, in the verse itself. That settles the "Burgess invented polar longitude" objection and gives Moira solid ground for a siddhantic polar family. The best-attested text-faithful ayanamsa in the whole corpus turns out to be one with no star at all: the Grahalaghava's one-line rule.
